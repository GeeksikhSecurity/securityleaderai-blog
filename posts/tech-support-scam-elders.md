# Tech Support Scams Target Our Elders — What Every Panjabi Family Needs to Know

*A phone screen says MICROSOFT SUPPORT. The voice says your computer has been hacked. Your parent, alone at home, has a minute to decide whether to trust them. Scammers have practiced this a thousand times. Your family hasn't.*

---

> **The one rule that stops this scam:** Real companies and government agencies will not phone you about your computer. If someone phones — hang up.

---

> **In short:** In 2024, the FBI recorded more than $924 million lost to computer scams, and most of the people targeted were over 60. Scammers pretend to be Microsoft, Apple, your bank, or the government. They use fake warnings on the screen, faked phone numbers, and fear to rush you. The safe move is simple: hang up, then phone the company yourself using a number you look up — never the number the stranger gives you.

---

## How the Scam Works

The scam follows the same four steps every time. Once you know them, it is easy to spot.

**Step 1 — The scare.** A warning appears on the computer screen and says the computer has a virus. It may play a loud alarm and show a phone number. The screen may freeze. This is not a real warning from Microsoft or Apple. It is a fake page made to look real. Real warnings never give you a phone number to call.

**Step 2 — The phone.** The person phones the number on the screen — or a stranger phones them out of the blue. The number your phone shows can be faked, so it may look real. The stranger sounds calm and helpful. Sometimes they speak Panjabi or Hindi so you feel at ease. They say they can fix the problem for you.

**Step 3 — Taking control.** The stranger asks the person to install a small program so they can "fix" the computer. Once it is installed, the stranger can control the computer from far away. Now they can see everything — bank pages, saved passwords, email. Never let a stranger control your computer.

**Step 4 — The payment.** The stranger says the repair costs money — often a few hundred dollars. They ask you to pay with gift cards (Amazon, Google Play, Apple) or a money transfer. They choose these because the money cannot be traced or returned. No real company asks to be paid this way.

---

## Six Warning Signs

Almost every scam of this kind uses one or more of these six tricks.

**1. They pretend to be someone you trust.** The stranger says they are from Microsoft, Apple, your bank, or the government. The name and number your phone shows can be faked. Remember: real companies will never phone you about your computer out of the blue.

**2. They play on your feelings.** "Your grandchildren's photos will be lost." "Your identity has been stolen." They use fear to rush you — especially with older people who feel less sure about computers.

**3. They rush you.** "You have 15 minutes before everything is deleted." No real company sets a deadline like this. The rush is there to stop you from thinking, or from phoning a family member.

**4. They offer a reward or make a threat.** Either "We'll fix it for free if you act now," or "If you don't pay, the police will come." Both are tricks. Real help never works this way.

**5. They show fake proof.** The stranger may point to "errors" on the screen to make the problem look real. These are normal messages that every computer has. They mean nothing.

**6. They ask to be paid in an odd way.** Gift cards, money transfers, cryptocurrency. No real company asks you to pay for computer help with a gift card. If someone asks you to scratch the back of a gift card and read them the numbers — it is a scam, every single time.

## What to Do If It Happens

**While you are on the phone:**

- Hang up right away. You do not owe a stranger politeness — just hang up.
- If a message froze the screen, hold the Power button to turn the computer off. Do not phone the number on the screen.

**If you already let them control the computer:**

- Turn off the internet right away (unplug the cable or switch off Wi-Fi).
- Phone your bank and ask them to freeze your accounts.
- Change your email and bank passwords from a different, safe device.
- Report it (see the list below).

## Where to Get Help and Report

If you are not sure whether something is a scam, you can phone a free helpline in your country. Real people will talk it through with you — you do not have to decide alone.

| Country   | Free helpline — call to ask or get help        | Where to report                                                                    |
| --------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| USA       | AARP Fraud Watch Network — **877-908-3360**    | FBI IC3 [ic3.gov](https://www.ic3.gov) · FTC [reportfraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov) |
| Canada    | Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre — **1-888-495-8501** | [antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca](https://antifraudcentre-centreantifraude.ca) |
| UK        | Citizens Advice consumer helpline — **0808 223 1133** | Action Fraud **0300 123 2040** · [reportfraud.police.uk](https://www.reportfraud.police.uk) |
| Australia | IDCARE — **1800 595 160** (also New Zealand)   | Scamwatch [scamwatch.gov.au](https://www.scamwatch.gov.au)                          |

You do not have to be a member of any of these to call. The helplines are free.
